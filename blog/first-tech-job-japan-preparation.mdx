Your first technology job in Japan is usually won through a combination of evidence: foundational knowledge, projects that show you can build and learn, communication suited to the team, and a focused application. A certificate can help a recruiter understand your starting point. It cannot replace a portfolio, an interview, or proof that you can work through an unfamiliar problem.

This guide is for new graduates, career changers, and junior developers. It explains what qualifications can help, what is actually required, and how to prepare for the Japanese hiring process without treating every company as identical.

<Highlight color="lemon">Read every job description as a specification. The required skills, language level, location, work authorisation, and interview process belong to that particular role—not to “tech jobs in Japan” as a whole.</Highlight>

## Start by choosing a realistic target role

“IT” covers a wide range of work. A first application becomes much stronger when it names a direction: frontend engineer, backend engineer, QA engineer, cloud support engineer, data analyst, implementation engineer, or junior software engineer.

Read 20 relevant postings and note repeated requirements. For example, a frontend lane may repeatedly mention TypeScript, React, browser debugging, accessibility, and design collaboration. A backend lane may emphasise Java, Python, SQL, APIs, testing, and cloud basics. QA roles may value test design, automation, bug reporting, and product thinking.

Then choose one primary stack to practise deeply enough to explain. A junior candidate does not need to know every framework. They need to show that they can learn a system, produce a useful change, and communicate the reasoning behind it.

## Qualifications: required, useful, and optional

There is no single certificate required for all entry-level technology roles in Japan. Employers may require a degree, specific experience, Japanese ability, or a work status that fits the position. Many product-engineering roles prioritise coding ability and projects; some domestic employers value national IT examinations because they provide a familiar baseline.

| Qualification | When it helps | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Degree, diploma, or relevant coursework | New-graduate recruitment and roles that specify academic requirements | That you can ship or operate a real application |
| IPA IT Passport (IP) | Business-facing IT, support, or an early foundation in IT concepts | Software-engineering ability |
| IPA Fundamental Information Technology Engineer (FE) | Strong entry-level signal for software, systems, and IT-engineering paths | Framework experience or a portfolio |
| IPA Information Security Management (SG) | Security-aware operations, support, governance, or compliance-adjacent roles | Hands-on security engineering |
| IPA Applied Information Technology Engineer (AP) | A later-stage broad signal after foundations are secure | A replacement for experience; it is not necessary for most first jobs |
| Cloud or platform certification | When the target role explicitly uses AWS, Azure, GCP, or a vendor platform | Architecture judgment or incident experience |
| JLPT | Roles where a written language credential helps demonstrate Japanese reading/listening ability | Speaking, writing, meeting facilitation, or workplace communication |

### IPA examinations

Japan's IPA runs a national Information Technology Engineers Examination framework. The [official overview](https://www.ipa.go.jp/en/it-examinations/reference.html) places IT Passport at Level 1, and Information Security Management and FE at Level 2. IPA itself describes FE as an entry point for IT engineers and says it covers the foundational knowledge and skills needed to help design, develop, integrate, and operate systems under guidance.

For a first software-engineering job, FE is often the most defensible exam target if you have the time and can take it in the required language. It gives you a structured way to learn algorithms, databases, networks, security, development process, and basic project concepts. If you are still exploring technology or aiming at a business-facing role, IP can be a sensible introduction. Do not collect both simply for the credential; choose the level that matches your target.

### Japanese-language qualifications

The JLPT has five levels, from N5 to N1. Its [official level guide](https://www.jlpt.jp/sp/e/about/levelsummary.html) describes N3 as a bridge between the lower and higher levels. Many Japanese-language job listings use N2 or N1 as a screening signal, while English-first companies may set different expectations. The actual requirement belongs to the posting.

The certificate has an important limit: the JLPT assesses language knowledge, reading, and listening, not speaking or writing interaction. The [official CEFR reference](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/about/cefr_reference.html) makes this explicit. Prepare separately to introduce a project, ask for clarification, explain a bug, and write a concise follow-up message in the language the team uses.

### Vendor certificates

AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Salesforce, and similar vendor credentials can help when they match the job. They are most useful after you have built a small deployment or lab that lets you explain the service choices. A generic cloud badge with no project is weaker than a small app whose deployment, environment variables, logs, access controls, and cost trade-offs you understand.

<Highlight color="flame">Do not spend months accumulating certificates to avoid applying. One relevant qualification plus a concrete project is usually stronger than a long list of unrelated badges.</Highlight>

## Build a portfolio that answers interview questions

For an entry-level applicant, a portfolio is evidence of how you work. Aim for two or three projects with different strengths rather than ten unfinished demos.

Each project page should make these points easy to find:

- The user problem and intended audience.
- Your contribution and the scope you personally owned.
- The technical choices and why they fit the problem.
- The data model, API, or key interaction.
- How you handled validation, errors, accessibility, security, or performance.
- What you tested and the risk those tests cover.
- What you would improve with more time.

Include a deployed demo when safe, a tidy repository, setup instructions, and screenshots or a short walkthrough. Do not claim production scale for a personal project. The credible story is: “I used this project to learn how a complete feature works, and here is what I learned when I made trade-offs.”

## Prepare Japanese-style application materials

Companies may ask for a resume, a Japanese-style 履歴書, a 職務経歴書 for experienced applicants, an entry sheet (ES), a coding profile, and links to GitHub or a portfolio. Follow the format requested by the company. A careful application shows that you can work with constraints.

Create a small application kit:

1. A one-page English resume and, where useful, a Japanese resume checked by a fluent reviewer.
2. A short project inventory: role, stack, link, outcome, and one technical challenge.
3. Three tailored motivation statements: why this company, why this role, and why you are ready now.
4. A clear statement of your Japanese and English working ability.
5. Links that work without private access or broken setup steps.

For a new-graduate schedule, timing matters. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's published example for employment in April 2026 shows publicity and entry activity beginning in March, interviews from June, and preliminary offers from October. Treat it as a general schedule, not a replacement for each employer's own deadline. Check company pages and your university career centre early.

## Search deliberately

Use several routes rather than relying on one job board: company career pages, university career services, technical communities, alumni, internships, reputable recruiters, and public employment services. For international students and foreign job seekers, Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare operates employment service centres in Tokyo, Nagoya, Osaka, and Fukuoka that provide guidance, job information, internships, and interview events.

Track applications in a simple sheet. Record the role, requirements, deadline, contact, tailored materials, interview stages, and feedback. After every rejection or interview, write one specific improvement: a clearer project explanation, stronger SQL practice, a better Japanese self-introduction, or more targeted applications.

If you need employer sponsorship or a status change, state your current situation accurately and check the role's requirements early. MHLW notes that work permission in Japan depends on residence status and permitted activity; for an offer, confirm the details with the employer and the relevant official immigration guidance rather than assuming eligibility.

## Rehearse for the interview formats you may meet

Most first-job technical interviews test a combination of motivation, communication, foundations, and problem-solving. A process may include an initial recruiter conversation, an aptitude or written assessment, a coding task, a technical interview, a team interview, and a final conversation. The sequence differs by employer.

Prepare these answers aloud:

| Interview prompt | What a strong answer includes |
| --- | --- |
| “Tell me about yourself.” | Present direction, relevant foundation, one project outcome, and why the role fits next. |
| “Why this company?” | A specific product, customer, technical challenge, or mission—not only the brand. |
| “Explain a project.” | Problem, your role, architecture, challenge, decision, result, and next improvement. |
| “Describe a failure.” | What happened, how you diagnosed it, what you changed, and what you learned. |
| “How would you approach this task?” | Clarify assumptions, identify users and constraints, propose a small plan, then discuss trade-offs. |

For coding interviews, practise reading a small problem carefully, clarifying inputs, naming trade-offs, and writing a simple correct solution before optimisation. For take-home tasks, respect the requested time limit, explain assumptions in the README, and submit work you can defend line by line. For Japanese interviews, rehearse a concise self-introduction and project explanation in Japanese; do not memorise paragraphs you cannot adapt to a follow-up question.

## A 12-week preparation plan

| Weeks | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Select target roles, analyse job descriptions, and identify one skills gap. |
| 3–6 | Build or improve one portfolio project; practise Git, tests, deployment, and clear documentation. |
| 7–8 | Prepare resumes, project stories, and language-specific self-introductions; seek feedback. |
| 9–10 | Apply to a focused set of roles; practise coding and behavioural interviews. |
| 11–12 | Review results, improve one weak area, follow up professionally, and continue the next application cycle. |

This plan is adaptable. A student may align it to recruitment seasons; a career changer may use it while working. Consistent output and reflection matter more than an ideal timetable.

## What employers should be able to see

By the time you apply, an employer should be able to see a direction, a foundation, and evidence of follow-through. Qualifications can make that story easier to understand. Your projects, explanations, and interview preparation make it believable.

<RainbowHighlight block>Your first tech job does not require a perfect profile—only a clear target, credible evidence of learning, and the preparation to explain how you will contribute.</RainbowHighlight>
