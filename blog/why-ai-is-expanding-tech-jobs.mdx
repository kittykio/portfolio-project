Cheaper software creation could make previously uneconomic projects worthwhile. It could also let a team deliver the same output with fewer people. Both mechanisms are plausible; neither settles the net effect on employment.

<Highlight color="lemon">Separate three questions: does a tool improve a task, does the organization build more software, and does it hire more people?</Highlight>

## Treat demand expansion as a scenario

Imagine a team spending 100 hours on an internal tool. If assistance reduces the complete delivery effort to 80 hours, the team might use the spare capacity for another tool, maintenance, or a smaller budget. The outcome depends on demand, funding, review capacity, and operational costs. These numbers illustrate a scenario; they are not measured productivity results.

The analogy with Jevons' paradox is useful for asking whether lower costs increase total demand. It is not a law proving that engineering headcount must rise. Code generation is only part of delivery; understanding the problem, validating behavior, and operating the service still consume effort.

## Read evidence at the right scale

The [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/software-developers.htm) publishes projections for developers, quality assurance analysts, and testers. Those projections concern defined occupations and a specific country. They are not proof that AI caused growth or a guarantee for a particular applicant.

A [METR randomized study published in July 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) found that the participating experienced open-source developers took longer on the studied tasks with the available AI tools. That result challenges a blanket speedup assumption; it does not establish that all developers or later tools become slower.

<Highlight color="flame">Do not turn one benchmark, hiring forecast, or company announcement into a universal prediction about jobs.</Highlight>

## Look at responsibilities rather than invented job titles

| Work | Useful evidence of skill |
| --- | --- |
| Define a feature | Clear acceptance criteria and non-goals |
| Integrate generated code | A reviewed diff with tested failure cases |
| Build an AI feature | A representative evaluation set and documented limits |
| Operate a service | Monitoring, recovery, and measured running costs |

These responsibilities can appear within existing roles. Some employers create dedicated evaluation or AI infrastructure positions; others distribute the work across product teams. A new responsibility is not automatically a new job opening.

## Make a practical learning plan

Choose one small feature and record time spent on design, implementation, review, and rework. Ask a teammate to inspect the result. Keep the same quality bar when comparing assisted and unassisted work; counting generated lines hides the cost of correction.

<Underline>Show what you can deliver and explain.</Underline> A portfolio case study can demonstrate an ambiguous requirement you clarified, a mistake your test caught, and a tradeoff you made. Continue practicing code reading and debugging so you can evaluate output without relying on the tool that produced it.

There is room for cautious optimism about useful software becoming easier to build. Confidence about any individual's career should come from adaptable skills and current opportunities, not a promise that technology makes employment risk disappear.

<RainbowHighlight block>Prepare for changing responsibilities while treating claims about net job growth as evidence to examine, not certainty.</RainbowHighlight>
