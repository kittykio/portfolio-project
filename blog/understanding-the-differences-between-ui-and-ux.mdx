A booking page can be visually polished and still leave someone wondering whether their reservation succeeded. That problem sits across the interface, the service, and the messages connecting them.

<Highlight color="lemon">UI is the interface people perceive and operate. UX includes their experience of reaching a goal through the whole service.</Highlight>

UI therefore includes interaction and feedback, not just decoration. UX includes what happens before and after a screen, not just how that screen feels. The responsibilities overlap; they are not separate stages completed by separate people.

## Follow one booking from start to finish

Suppose someone wants a table for four at 7 p.m. Interface decisions include how dates are selected, how unavailable slots are identified, and where keyboard focus appears. Experience decisions include whether availability is current, whether an account is necessary, and how a booking can be cancelled.

| Moment | Interface question | Wider experience question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Choose a time | Can users distinguish selected and unavailable slots? | Is availability accurate? |
| Enter details | Are fields labelled and errors linked to them? | Are all requested details necessary? |
| Submit | Does the button show progress? | Can retries create duplicate reservations? |
| Finish | Is the confirmation clear? | Can users retrieve or cancel their booking later? |

The table describes discussion prompts, not a rigid division of job titles. A developer implementing retries and a designer writing confirmation copy are both shaping the experience.

## Design beyond the happy path

Make a prototype with a fully booked time, a slow response, and a failed submission. Keep the entered details when recovery is possible. Explain what the user can do next without claiming success before the server confirms it.

<Underline>A visible state needs a meaningful explanation.</Underline> A red border without text leaves people guessing. A spinner without any recovery path can trap them. A disabled button may prevent duplicate clicks but cannot, by itself, make a server operation idempotent.

## Observe a task instead of asking for approval

Give a participant a specific scenario and ask them to complete it without coaching. Record where they hesitate, what they expect, and whether they can explain the final result. Small sessions reveal problems to investigate; they do not establish population-wide success rates.

Pair observations with operational evidence. A completed form is not necessarily a completed reservation. Track confirmed bookings and support requests about missing confirmations, while minimizing the personal data collected.

The [W3C forms tutorial](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/) is a useful implementation reference for labels, instructions, and feedback. Accessibility belongs in the interaction from the start, including keyboard use and understandable recovery.

## Choose the next improvement

Fix the point that prevents completion before polishing a decorative animation. Then re-run the same task. This makes a design decision reviewable: someone who previously could not recover from an error now can.

<RainbowHighlight block>A strong interface makes the next action clear, and a strong experience makes the whole task work.</RainbowHighlight>
